**Short answer:** **model space** is the drawing itself, drawn at real size — a wall is 4000 units long, not 40. **Paper space** (a _layout_) is the sheet of paper it will be printed on: the title block, the border, and one or more **viewports** — windows onto the model, each set to a scale like 1:100. You draw in model space; you arrange, annotate the sheet and plot from paper space. `MS` and `PS` (or a double-click) move you between them.

CADCraft works the same way: layouts, viewports, per-viewport layer freeze and plotting layouts to PDF all behave like AutoCAD's, and the examples below come from its sample drawings.

## Two spaces, one drawing

The tabs at the bottom-left of the window — **Model**, **Layout1**, **Layout2**… — are not files. They are views of the _same_ drawing database. Model space holds the geometry. Each layout is a sheet: it has a paper size, and it can hold viewports plus anything you'd draw on a sheet of paper.

The mistake this division prevents is the one every self-taught drafter makes first: drawing the title block around the plan in model space, then scaling the whole world down to fit an A3. That works until something has to change — and then every dimension, every piece of text and the border all need re-scaling by hand. The layout system exists so the model never has to know what paper is.

## Model space: everything at 1:1

- One unit equals one real unit. In a metric building drawing that's usually **millimetres** (a room is 4000, not 4); in mechanical work it may be metres or inches. Pick the unit once, in `UNITS`, and stay consistent — this is also what [DXF vs DWG](/blog/dxf-vs-dwg) round trips assume.
- Never draw something "at 1:50". The scale lives on the layout, not in the model.
- Set up layers for the real things — walls, doors, furniture, dimensions — as in [how AutoCAD layers work](/blog/autocad-layers-off-freeze-lock).

## Paper space: the sheet as itself

A layout **is** the paper. An A3 layout is 420 × 297 units — real millimetres of paper. That's why the title block, drawn on the layout, is the only thing in a CAD drawing that you draw _at the size it prints_: 5 mm text on the title block is 5 mm on paper.

Everything on a layout either belongs to the sheet (border, title block, north arrow, general notes) or is a **viewport**.

## Viewports: windows onto the model

`MV` (MVIEW) draws a rectangle on the layout; inside it, the model appears.

- **Double-click inside** the viewport: you are now in model space, _through_ the viewport — you can pan and edit, and the crosshair shows inside the viewport only. Double-click outside (or `PS`) to return to the sheet. `MS` jumps in without the double-click.
- **Set the scale:** select the viewport by clicking its border, then choose from the scale dropdown (status bar or Properties): **1:100** for plans, **1:50**, **1:20** for details. The classic keyboard form is `Z` `1/50xp` inside the viewport.
- **Lock it.** An unlocked viewport loses its scale the moment anyone zooms inside it. Click the padlock by the scale dropdown (or `VPLOCK`) — you can still pan inside a locked viewport, but the scale is fixed.

![A paper-space layout in practice: an A3 sheet with a title block down the right edge and two dashed viewports — the large one shows the apartment plan at 1:100 with its display locked, the lower one shows the kitchen at 1:20. One layout can carry as many viewports as the sheet usefully holds — a 1:100 overall plan and a run of 1:20 details on the same sheet is completely normal.

## Text and dimensions: which space?

Two workable schools:

- **Annotate on the layout.** Text and dimensions drawn in paper space are paper-sized — 2.5 mm text is 2.5 mm. Simple, and it suits drawings where the annotation belongs to the sheet rather than the geometry.
- **Annotate in model space with annotative styles.** AutoCAD's annotative text and dimensions resize themselves to come out right at the viewport scale, and CADCraft supports the same idea. Better when the annotation belongs to the model and several viewports show it.

What doesn't work is plain model-space text at an invented size: that's the hand-scaling trap again. [How to scale in AutoCAD](/blog/how-to-scale-in-autocad) covers the arithmetic if you want to do it by hand.

## Different layers in different viewports

Freeze a layer **per viewport** (the VP Freeze column in the Layer Properties Manager, or `VPLAYER`): the furniture layer can be frozen in the kitchen-detail viewport and visible in the overall plan, from one model. [Off vs Freeze vs Lock](/blog/autocad-layers-off-freeze-lock) has the full picture — this is a Freeze capability, not an Off one.

## Plot from the layout

With the sheet composed, plotting stops being guesswork: `PLOT` (Ctrl+P), paper **A3**, plot scale **1:1** — the sheet is already the right size, so there is nothing left to scale. Add a plot style table such as `monochrome.ctb` for black-line PDF output. The full walk-through, including the dialog settings that matter, is [DWG to PDF](/blog/dwg-to-pdf).

The one thing left that still needs care is **lineweights**: color-dependent plot styles map layer colors to pen widths, so the drawing's layer colors decide the printed line weights.

## When to skip layouts

For a quick print of a sketch, plotting model space with **Fit to paper** is fine — nothing depends on it. The moment a drawing will be issued, measured from, or revised, compose it on a layout: the scale is stated on the sheet, and the sheet is what prints.
