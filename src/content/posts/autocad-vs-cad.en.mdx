**Short answer:** **CAD** (computer-aided design) is a **category** of software for making precise drawings and models. **AutoCAD** is **one product** in that category, made by Autodesk since 1982, used mostly for 2D drafting. Every AutoCAD drawing is CAD; most CAD software isn't AutoCAD. It's the same relationship as "spreadsheet" and "Excel".

People mix them up because AutoCAD was, for decades, the CAD program most offices used, so "do it in CAD" and "do it in AutoCAD" came to mean the same thing.

## What CAD means

CAD is any software that replaces the drawing board for technical work: exact lengths and angles, layers, dimensions, standard symbols, and output a workshop or a builder can follow. You'll also see **CADD** (computer-aided design and drafting) and job titles like "CAD technician" or "CAD drafter", which describe the work, not a particular program.

It started long before AutoCAD. Ivan Sutherland's **Sketchpad** (1963) let you draw on a screen with a light pen and constrain lines. Through the 1970s, CAD ran on mainframes and expensive workstations in car, aircraft and electronics companies.

## Where AutoCAD fits

Autodesk released AutoCAD in December 1982. Its breakthrough was running on an ordinary **personal computer**, which put CAD within reach of small architecture and engineering offices. Its file format, **DWG**, became the de facto standard for exchanging 2D drawings, which is a big reason it's still everywhere.

Today AutoCAD is:

- **A 2D drafting program first:** plans, sections, details, schematics, layouts with title blocks, plotting to PDF.
- **Capable of 3D** (solids, surfaces, meshes), though most 3D design work happens in other tools.
- **Subscription only.** Autodesk stopped selling new perpetual licences in 2016. AutoCAD LT is the cheaper, 2D-only edition.
- **The base of a family:** AutoCAD Architecture, Mechanical, Electrical, MEP, Map 3D and Plant 3D are "toolsets" on top of it, and Civil 3D builds on it too.

## The main types of CAD, and where AutoCAD isn't the usual choice

| Type              | What you make                                          | Typical tools                                                   |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **2D drafting**   | Plans, details, schematics, layouts for laser cutting  | AutoCAD, AutoCAD LT, BricsCAD, DraftSight, CADCraft, LibreCAD   |
| **3D parametric** | Mechanical parts and assemblies, driven by dimensions  | SolidWorks, Inventor, Fusion, CATIA, Creo, NX, FreeCAD, Onshape |
| **BIM**           | A building model that produces plans, schedules and 3D | Revit, ArchiCAD, Vectorworks, Bonsai, FreeCAD BIM               |
| **ECAD**          | Circuit schematics and printed circuit boards          | KiCad, Altium, Autodesk's Fusion electronics                    |
| **Civil / GIS**   | Roads, grading, surveys, maps                          | Civil 3D, QGIS                                                  |
| **Code CAD**      | Parts defined in a script                              | OpenSCAD, CadQuery                                              |

So "AutoCAD vs CAD" often really means "AutoCAD vs another kind of CAD":

- **AutoCAD vs SolidWorks or Inventor:** AutoCAD draws geometry; parametric tools build parts from features and constraints, then generate the 2D drawings. For machine design, the parametric tool wins.
- **AutoCAD vs Revit:** AutoCAD draws lines that look like walls; Revit models walls that know they're walls, so sections and schedules update themselves. Architects increasingly use BIM, with AutoCAD for details and for exchange with consultants.
- **AutoCAD vs other 2D CAD:** here the programs are close. BricsCAD and DraftSight are commercial DWG-based alternatives; CADCraft, LibreCAD and QCAD are free. [Free AutoCAD alternatives](/blog/free-autocad-alternatives) compares them.

## Do AutoCAD skills transfer to other CAD?

Mostly, yes:

- **To other 2D CAD:** almost entirely. Command names, layers, object snaps, blocks and plotting work the same way in BricsCAD, DraftSight and CADCraft. Even the aliases (`L` for LINE, `TR` for TRIM) carry over; see the [AutoCAD commands cheat sheet](/blog/autocad-commands).
- **To 3D parametric and BIM:** partly. Drafting conventions, scale and precision carry over; the way of working (sketches and features, or walls and families) is new and takes weeks to learn.
- **The other direction:** someone who knows SolidWorks or Revit usually finds AutoCAD easy to pick up for 2D, and [AutoCAD shortcuts](/blog/autocad-shortcuts) is a quick start.

## Which do you need?

- **A job ad says "CAD":** check the industry. Architecture and construction often mean AutoCAD or Revit; mechanical engineering means SolidWorks, Inventor or similar; electronics means an ECAD tool.
- **You need 2D drawings and don't want a subscription:** a free 2D CAD program. [CADCraft](/download) feels the most like AutoCAD; [try it in the browser](/online) without installing.
- **You need to exchange files with AutoCAD users:** any tool that reads and writes **DWG** or **DXF**. [DXF vs DWG](/blog/dxf-vs-dwg) explains the difference.
- **You're a student:** get the free Autodesk education licence, and learn the concepts (layers, snaps, scale, dimensions) that every CAD program shares.
